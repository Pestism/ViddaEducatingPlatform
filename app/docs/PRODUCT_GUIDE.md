# Vidda Solutions — Product Guide
## What Each Step Does and Why It Exists

---

## The Core Idea

A bank employs hundreds of people in AML and compliance roles. Every one of them needs training. But most compliance training is generic — the same course for a KYC Analyst and an MLRO, covering the same material, tested with the same quiz.

This is a problem for two reasons. First, it does not actually prepare people for their specific job. A Customer Advisor does not need to know how to file a SAR — they need to know how to spot unusual behaviour at onboarding. An MLRO does not need to know basic KYC — they need to understand when accumulated risk indicators cross the threshold that requires them personally to act, with personal criminal liability on the line.

Second, generic training cannot be defended. If a regulator asks "how do you know your MLRO understood the escalation obligation?" and the answer is "they passed a generic AML quiz," that is not a satisfying answer.

Vidda Solutions exists to fix both problems. It generates training that is built for a specific person in a specific role, traces every decision back to the regulation that demands it, keeps a human manager in the loop throughout, and produces a permanent audit trail that can be handed to a regulator as proof.

The platform works in seven steps. Each step has a purpose. Each step produces something that feeds into the next.

## STEP 1 — Select Role

### What it is

The starting point. A manager chooses which role they are building a training programme for.

### The five roles available

**MLRO — Money Laundering Reporting Officer**
The most senior and most exposed AML role in the bank. The MLRO is personally accountable for every Suspicious Activity Report decision. If the bank fails to report a case of money laundering because the MLRO did not act, the MLRO can face criminal charges — not just the institution. This role requires judgment training, not just knowledge training.

**KYC Analyst**
The analyst who verifies customer identities and assesses ML/TF risk during onboarding and ongoing review. They work with the bank's highest-risk customers directly. If they approve a customer whose identity is fraudulent or whose ownership structure hides a criminal, that customer is now inside the bank. Detection failures at this stage are irreversible.

**Transaction Monitoring Analyst**
The analyst who reviews alerts generated by automated transaction monitoring systems. They see high volumes of alerts and must decide which ones represent genuine suspicion and which are false positives. The risk here is fatigue — missing a real case because of alert overload — and inadequate documentation when they do escalate.

**Customer Advisor**
Front-line staff who are often the first human contact a suspicious customer has with the bank. They are not AML specialists, but they are expected to recognise unusual behaviour and escalate it. Their risk is different — it is not depth of knowledge, it is knowing when something feels wrong and having the confidence to raise it.

**AML DDI Manager**
Manages the AML and KYC checks on the bank's delivery partners and third parties. The risk here is that the bank's exposure to money laundering does not only come through its own customers — it comes through everyone its partners deal with. This role also has coaching responsibilities, meaning any training gap in this person propagates to the analysts they supervise.

### Why this step matters

The role chosen here determines everything that follows. The responsibilities, the risk exposures, the regulatory obligations, the training modules, the scenario — all of it is derived from this single selection. This is what makes the training role-specific rather than generic.

## STEP 2 — Analyse

### What it is

The system analyses the selected role and produces a structured picture of what that person does, what risks they face, and what the regulation requires of them. This is the AI doing the heavy lifting before any human has to review anything.

### What the system does

The platform pulls two bodies of evidence together. First, it searches for everything relevant to this specific role — their responsibilities, the kinds of decisions they make, the financial crime risks attached to their position. Second, it searches the actual text of the AMLR (Anti-Money Laundering Regulation 1624) to find which articles directly apply to someone in this role.

It then uses this combined evidence to generate a seven-part framework — a structured analysis of the role across seven dimensions that will become the basis for human review in the next step.

At the same time, it generates a full training curriculum: a set of modules spread across four quarters, each with a name, an objective, a time estimate, and — critically — a documented reason why it exists for this specific role.

### What gets produced

A complete, structured analysis covering:
- What this person's job actually is (not a job description — a functional analysis)
- The specific responsibilities they carry that create AML exposure
- The inherent risk level of each exposure (Critical, High, Medium, Low)
- The themes that run through their risk profile
- Which AMLR articles create legal obligations for someone in this role
- What knowledge, skills, and judgment they need
- What a training programme for them should look like across a year

### Why this step matters

Without analysis, training is guesswork. This step makes the training programme defensible — every module that gets assigned later exists because the analysis identified a specific need tied to a specific risk tied to a specific regulation. That chain of reasoning is what the manager reviews in Step 3.

## STEP 3 — Validate

### What it is

The human review step. A manager looks at everything the AI produced in Step 2 and decides whether it is accurate, needs adjustment, or should be rejected.

This is not a rubber stamp. The manager has real control.

### What the manager sees

Seven expandable panels — one for each dimension of the analysis. Each panel shows what the AI concluded and the evidence it drew on.

**Panel 1: Role Function**
What is this person's primary job? What department are they in? The manager checks this against their knowledge of the actual role.

**Panel 2: Main Responsibilities**
Every specific responsibility the analysis identified, with the supporting evidence. The manager checks that nothing important is missing and nothing irrelevant has been included.

**Panel 3: Inherent AML Risk**
Each risk exposure the role carries, rated Critical, High, Medium, or Low. The manager can see the reasoning behind each rating. This is where the analysis becomes most specific to the role — the risk profile of an MLRO is radically different from that of a Customer Advisor.

**Panel 4: Risk Themes**
The broad themes that tie the individual risks together. These become the organising logic of the training curriculum.

**Panel 5: Regulatory Obligations**
Which AMLR articles apply. Clicking any article number opens the actual article text from the regulation, with the relevant passages highlighted. The manager is not being asked to trust the system's claim — they can read the source.

**Panel 6: Competencies (Knowledge, Skills, Judgment)**
What the person needs to know, what they need to be able to do, and what quality of judgment they need to develop. These three categories come from the K/S/J competency framework for AML professionals.

**Panel 7: Training Focus**
How the curriculum is structured across Q1–Q4, and how many modules land in each quarter.

### What the manager can do

For each panel, the manager has three choices:

**Approve** — the analysis is accurate, move on.

**Modify** — the analysis needs adjustment. The manager edits the content directly — changing a responsibility description, adjusting a risk rating, correcting a regulatory mapping. The edited version replaces the AI output. The panel shows a "Modified" badge so the change is visible.

**Reject** — the panel is wrong and needs to be reconsidered. Rejected panels cannot be approved until they are reset and re-reviewed.

All seven panels must be approved before the training path can be generated. There is no way to skip this step.

### Why this step matters

This is the governance gate. The AI produces a first draft. A human with domain knowledge reviews it. Nothing moves forward without human sign-off.

This matters for two reasons. First, AI can be wrong. A manager who knows the role can catch errors the system would not catch. Second, the audit trail. When a training programme is assigned and an employee is trained, the record shows that a named manager reviewed and approved each element of the analysis. That accountability is what makes the downstream training defensible.

## STEP 4 — Training Path

### What it is

The full curriculum for this role, laid out across four quarters. This is the output of the approved analysis — all the decisions made in Steps 2 and 3 translated into a concrete set of training modules with clear explainability for each one.

### What a training module looks like

Every module has a name, a quarter it sits in, a duration, and a content type (e-learning, workshop, case study, or simulation). But what makes this different from a standard course catalogue is the explainability attached to every module:

**Reason assigned** — a plain-English explanation of why this specific module was included for this specific role. Not "AML awareness is important" but "the MLRO carries personal liability for SAR decisions; this module addresses the judgment dimension of recognising when the suspicion threshold has been crossed."

**Risk alignment** — which of the role's identified risk exposures this module directly addresses.

**AMLR articles** — which specific articles of the regulation this module is designed to satisfy.

**Competencies** — what knowledge, skills, and judgment the module develops.

### Why this step matters

The training path is not a list of courses. It is a documented curriculum with a documented rationale for every element. This answers the question regulators actually ask: not "do you have a training programme?" but "can you explain why your MLRO received training on beneficial ownership structures?" The answer is in the module record.

## STEP 5 — Generate Training Material

### What it is

The vertical slice. The manager selects one module from the training path and generates the actual study content for it. This is the step that bridges curriculum planning and real learning — turning a module entry in a training plan into content an employee can actually study.

### Why only one module

The training path might contain 15–20 modules. Building the full content for all of them would take weeks. The platform demonstrates the capability on one module — showing that the content generated is genuinely role-specific, regulation-grounded, and ready for review. The same process could be applied to all modules.

### What the generated material contains

For the selected module, the platform generates:

**Learning objectives** — three to five specific, measurable things the employee should be able to do after completing this module.

**Plain-English summary** — a short overview of what the module covers and why it matters for this role. Written in clear, accessible language — not regulatory jargon.

**Key concepts** — the four to six essential ideas the employee needs to understand. Short phrases, not paragraphs.

**Role-specific example** — a realistic scenario drawn from the actual risk profile of this role, showing how the concepts apply in practice. An MLRO example looks completely different from a Customer Advisor example, even for a module with the same name.

**Regulatory traceability** — the AMLR articles this module satisfies, written as explicit citations. This is the link between the learning content and the legal obligation.

**Knowledge check** — two questions that test understanding, with four options each, the correct answer, and an explanation of why it is correct.

**Manager review note** — a one-sentence prompt flagging what the manager should verify before approving the material for assignment.

### The manager's role here

The manager reads the generated material, checks the role-specific example against their institution's actual procedures, and either approves it or regenerates it. An employee will never see material that has not been approved by a manager. The approval step is required before the training can be assigned in Step 6.

### Why this step matters

This is where the platform closes the gap that most compliance training platforms leave open. A training plan tells you what an employee should learn. Training material is what they actually learn. Generating it from the same role profile, risk analysis, and regulatory mapping that produced the curriculum means the content is coherent, specific, and traceable from first principle to final word.

## STEP 6 — LMS Assignment

### What it is

The assignment step. The manager formally assigns the approved training programme to a specific employee by entering their email address. This is the handoff from planning to delivery.

### What the manager sees

Before assigning, the manager sees a summary of everything that has been built:
- How many modules are in the training path
- How many are mandatory
- The total study hours across the year
- Which AMLR article range the programme covers

They also see the complete audit trail of every decision made from Step 1 to this point — role imported, analysis run, each checkpoint approved, material generated and approved. This is the governance record of how the training programme was built.

### What happens when training is assigned

The employee's email is recorded as the designated learner. Everything that happens in Step 7 — every scenario completed, every decision made, every justification submitted — is logged against this email address. The assignment is the moment the programme becomes the employee's.

### Why this step matters

Assignment creates accountability in both directions. The manager has formally committed to this training programme for this employee. The employee, once they complete the scenario in Step 7, has a permanent record tied to their identity. Neither can later claim the training did not happen or that the content was not reviewed.

## STEP 7 — Monitor Training Activity

### What it is

The final step, and the one that proves the training worked. The manager monitors the employee's activity in real time while the employee works through a realistic compliance scenario.

This step has two views on the same screen: the manager's dashboard at the top, and the employee's scenario below.

### The manager's dashboard

The manager can see at a glance:
- Whether the employee has started, is in progress, or has completed
- How many modules they have worked through
- The exact decision the employee made in the scenario
- The justification the employee wrote in their own words
- The timestamp of when the activity was logged

This is live. It updates the moment the employee submits. The manager does not need to check in, follow up, or ask for a completion report.

### The employee's scenario

The employee works through a realistic decision scenario suited to their role. For the MLRO role, the scenario involves a corporate customer who has been escalated from KYC for a final onboarding decision. The case file contains multiple red flags. The employee reviews the evidence, selects a course of action, writes their reasoning, and submits.

For a full explanation of the scenario — what the case involves, what each piece of evidence means, what the correct decision is and why — see the separate Scenario Simulation document.

### What happens after the employee submits

Two things happen simultaneously.

First, the decision and justification are permanently recorded. The record includes which evidence the employee reviewed, what they decided, what they wrote, whether their decision was correct, which AMLR articles apply, and what competency was being tested. This record cannot be edited or deleted.

Second, the employee's written justification is evaluated for audit-readiness. The platform assesses whether the justification references the evidence reviewed, explains the decision clearly, uses the right risk terminology, and is consistent with the recommended course of action. The employee sees a score, a verdict on whether their justification is audit-ready, a list of what was missing, and a suggested improved version.

This feedback is not punitive. It is developmental. It shows the employee not just that they got the answer right or wrong, but whether the way they documented their reasoning would hold up in a regulatory review — and what a strong justification looks like.

### Why this step matters

Training that cannot be measured is not training — it is exposure. This step measures two things that actually matter:

First, whether the employee can make the right call in a realistic situation. Not whether they can recognise the correct answer in a multiple-choice list, but whether they can read a case, weigh the evidence, and decide.

Second, whether they can explain their reasoning in a way that is audit-ready. The ability to make good decisions and the ability to document those decisions in a defensible way are both required competencies in an AML role. This step tests both.

The permanent record produced here is the final piece of the governance chain. The bank can demonstrate that a specific employee, assigned to a specific role, received training built specifically for that role, reviewed and approved by a named manager, completed a realistic scenario assessment, had their reasoning evaluated, and produced a documented outcome — all traceable to specific articles of the regulation that makes this training legally required.

## The Complete Governance Chain

Each step feeds the next. Nothing can be skipped.

| Step | Who acts | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Select Role | Manager | Role definition and risk profile |
| 2. Analyse | System (AI + RAG) | 7-part framework analysis + curriculum |
| 3. Validate | Manager | Human-approved analysis with full edit trail |
| 4. Training Path | System (AI) | Explainable, regulation-mapped curriculum |
| 5. Generate Material | System + Manager | Role-specific content, manager-approved |
| 6. Assign | Manager | Formal assignment to named employee |
| 7. Monitor | Employee + System | Scenario result, justification, AI evaluation, audit record |

At the end of this chain, the bank has something it can put in front of a regulator: a complete, documented training programme for a specific role, with a specific employee's assessment record attached, traceable from the first role selection to the last submitted justification.

That is what governance looks like in practice.
