# Scenario Simulation — What It Is and Why It Exists

---

## The Problem It Solves

Most compliance training ends with a multiple-choice quiz. The employee clicks answers, scores 80%, gets a certificate, and the bank ticks a box. But that certificate does not prove the employee can actually make a defensible decision under pressure — and a regulator asking "can you show me that your MLRO understood this risk?" cannot be satisfied by a tick-box certificate.

The scenario simulation answers a different question:

> **Can this employee recognise a real situation, weigh the evidence, make the right call, and explain their reasoning in a way that would survive regulatory scrutiny?**

---

## What the Scenario Is

The employee is placed in the role of an MLRO (Money Laundering Reporting Officer) who has just received an escalation from the KYC team.

**The case: Northwind Holdings Ltd**

A corporate client has applied to open an account. KYC has reviewed the application and cannot make the call themselves — they have escalated it to the MLRO for a final decision. The MLRO must decide whether to approve the onboarding, reject it, ask for more information, or escalate internally and file a suspicious activity report.

This is a real type of decision that MLROs face. There is no theoretical question here. The employee is not being asked "what does Article 9 say?" — they are being asked to act.

---

## What the Employee Does

The employee works through the case in four stages:

**1. Read the brief**
A short paragraph explains who the client is, what KYC found, and why it was escalated. The employee reads this to understand the situation.

**2. Review the evidence**
There are five evidence items in the case file. Each one is a specific finding — something KYC discovered during the review. The employee clicks to open each one and reads what was found and why it matters.

The five findings in this case are:
- The ownership chain ends at a nominee company in BVI with no declaration of who actually owns the business
- Two entities in the ownership chain are in FATF high-risk jurisdictions, and the operating bank accounts are in a third high-risk country
- Adverse media screening found a possible match against a foreign political figure with corruption allegations — unresolved at 78% confidence
- Six large wire transfers arrived within 48 hours of account opening, each just below the internal review threshold — a classic structuring pattern
- The stated source of funds is "private investment proceeds" with no supporting documentation whatsoever

**3. Choose a decision**
The employee selects one of four options:
- Approve the onboarding
- Reject the onboarding
- Request more information from the client
- Escalate internally and file a Suspicious Activity Report

**4. Write a justification**
The employee must write their reasoning in their own words before they can submit. This is the most important part. The justification is what would go into the case file. It is what a regulator or auditor would read. It has to be coherent, reference the evidence, and explain the thinking.

---

## What the Employee Sees After Submitting

Two things appear immediately:

**Explainability feedback**
The platform tells the employee whether their decision was correct, acceptable, or wrong — and crucially, explains why in plain language. It also tells them:
- What the recommended action was and why
- Which AMLR regulation articles apply to this situation
- What competency the scenario was testing (knowledge, skill, or judgment)
- What the real-world consequence would be if they made the wrong call

**Justification evaluation**
The employee's written justification is evaluated for audit-readiness. The platform assesses four things:
- Did they reference the specific evidence they reviewed?
- Did they explain their reasoning clearly?
- Did they use the right risk terminology (UBO, PEP, structuring, source of funds)?
- Is their explanation consistent with the correct course of action?

The employee gets a score out of 100, a clear verdict on whether their justification is audit-ready, a list of what is missing, and a suggested improved version that shows them what a strong justification looks like.

---

## What the Correct Answer Is — and Why Each Option Is Different

**Escalate and file an internal SAR — Correct**
All five red flags together cross the suspicion threshold. The MLRO has both the legal authority and the duty to act. Filing an internal SAR starts the proper investigative process and creates the paper trail that protects the institution and the individual.

**Reject — Acceptable**
Refusing to onboard the client is a defensible choice. But silently declining without filing a SAR means the suspicion is not recorded. The duty to report suspicion of money laundering is a separate legal obligation from the commercial decision — declining alone does not satisfy it.

**Request more information — Acceptable**
In many cases this would be the right first step. But here, the transaction structuring pattern that appeared within 48 hours of account opening is already a reportable suspicion. Asking the client for more documents must be done carefully because telling a client that a SAR is being considered is a criminal offence (tipping-off).

**Approve — Incorrect**
None of the legal requirements for establishing this business relationship have been met. The UBO is unknown, the PEP match is unresolved, the source of funds is unverifiable, and the transaction pattern suggests structuring. Approving would expose the institution to regulatory enforcement and expose the MLRO personally to criminal liability.

---

## Why the Justification Matters More Than the Decision

The decision is important. But in a real regulatory review, the question is not just "what did you decide?" — it is "why did you decide it, and can you prove you understood the risk?"

A justification that says "I rejected because it looked risky" is not audit-ready. A justification that says "I escalated based on the unresolved UBO chain terminating at a BVI nominee, the unconfirmed PEP match, the threshold-avoidance structuring pattern, and the absence of source-of-funds documentation — each of which individually warrants enhanced due diligence under Article 9, and collectively meets the suspicion threshold for internal SAR" is audit-ready.

The platform teaches the difference. It shows the employee what was missing from their justification, and it shows them what a strong one looks like — so the next time they write one, it is better.

---

## What the Manager Sees

While the employee is going through the scenario, the manager's dashboard at the top of the screen shows:
- Whether training has started, is in progress, or is complete
- How many modules have been completed
- The last decision the employee made
- The first line of the justification they submitted
- The exact time the activity was logged

The manager does not need to ask the employee "did you do your training?" — they can see it in real time, with the evidence already attached.

---

## What Gets Recorded

Every submission creates a permanent record that contains:
- Which employee completed the scenario
- Which evidence they opened and reviewed
- What decision they made
- What they wrote as their justification
- Whether it was the correct call
- Which AMLR regulations apply
- What competency was being assessed
- The exact timestamp

This record exists whether the employee did well or poorly. If a regulator asks the bank to demonstrate that its MLRO received scenario-based training on corporate onboarding risk and was assessed on their ability to recognise a suspicious case — this record is the answer.

---

## The Bigger Picture

The scenario simulation is the final proof point in the platform's governance chain:

1. The training was built around this specific role — not a generic course
2. The training was mapped to the actual regulation that applies to this role
3. A manager reviewed and approved the training before it was assigned
4. The employee received the training material
5. The employee was tested in a realistic scenario, not a multiple-choice quiz
6. Their reasoning was evaluated for audit-readiness, not just their answer
7. Everything was logged, timestamped, and stored permanently

That is a training programme that can be defended in front of a regulator. That is the point of the platform.
